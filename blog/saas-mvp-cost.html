If you are planning a product, the SaaS MVP cost is usually the first number you need and the hardest one to get straight. Most guides give you a range like $30,000 to $80,000 and stop there. That does not help you decide what to build first or what to cut.

I build SaaS products and AI features as a full-stack developer, including CultureAI, a multi-tenant employee engagement platform, and BWL League, a multi-role sports platform. In this guide I break the cost down by feature, show what an MVP costs to run every month after launch, compare freelancers, agencies and no-code tools, and share the cuts that save money without making the product worse.

SaaS MVP cost in 2026: the short answer

Published estimates vary a lot, mostly because people mean different things by "MVP". Here is what the current guides say, grouped by who builds it.

Who builds itTypical rangeWhat you usually get
Agency$30,000 to $80,000 for a narrow MVPDiscovery, design, development, QA and project management
Senior US freelancer at $100 to $150 an hour$50,000 to $90,000Experienced development, usually without a full team
Mid-level freelancer at $40 to $60 an hour$16,000 to $30,000Development, with design and management often on you
Lean build with a tiny scopeA few thousand dollars or lessLogin, a basic dashboard and one core workflow

The agency range comes from Purrweb, and the freelancer figures come from WeAreFounders. Treat them as rough benchmarks, because most were written by companies that sell this work, including me.

Location matters as much as skill. I work from Karachi, so my rates sit well below US or European freelancers for the same stack. My own MVP package starts at $399 for the smallest useful version: login, user roles, a dashboard and one core feature. That is a deliberately narrow first version for testing one idea, not a full product. Once billing, integrations or AI enter the picture, I quote after a short scope call.

What drives the cost: a feature-by-feature breakdown

A quote is really a list of features multiplied by a day rate. Once you see the list, the big range above starts to make sense. These are my own estimates for a solo senior developer building a typical B2B SaaS MVP with modern tools.

FeatureTypical effortWhat makes it grow
Sign-up, login and password reset1 to 3 daysSocial login, single sign-on, team invites
User roles and permissions2 to 5 daysEvery extra role and every shared record
The core workflow customers pay for5 to 15 daysBusiness rules and edge cases
Dashboard3 to 7 daysCharts, filters and exports
Stripe subscriptions and webhooks3 to 6 daysPer-seat pricing, trials and plan changes
One AI feature3 to 8 daysRetrieval over your own data, agents, testing
Emails and notifications1 to 3 daysSlack, WhatsApp or SMS channels
Simple admin tools2 to 5 daysSupport tools and reporting
Deployment, monitoring and backups1 to 3 daysMultiple environments and compliance

That adds up to roughly 21 to 55 working days. At 8 hours a day, the same scope costs about $4,200 to $11,000 at $25 an hour, $10,000 to $26,000 at $60 an hour, and $25,000 to $66,000 at $150 an hour. The work is the same; the rate changes the total.

Where the hidden days go

In my projects, two features take longer than founders expect: roles and billing.

On BWL League, admins, coaches and teams each see different data. Every extra role meant new access rules in Supabase, new screens and more tests. A single-role product is much cheaper to build than a three-role one.

On CultureAI, the checkout page was the easy part. Keeping seats, invoices and access in sync was the real work, which is why I wrote up what actually breaks in Stripe per-seat billing. If your pricing depends on seats or usage, add days to the billing line.

AI features: cheap demo, expensive reliability

Getting an AI demo working takes an afternoon. Making it reliable takes longer: structured output, validation, usage limits and a fallback when the model fails. In CultureAI, the AI summaries return structured JSON so the app can check the result before showing it. Budget for that work, not just the API call.

Design, content and testing

The table assumes a clean, standard interface built from a component library. A custom brand design, a marketing website and written onboarding content are separate jobs, so ask whether a quote includes them before you compare prices.

Testing is the easiest line to cut and the most expensive one to skip. Before launch, every role, every billing event and every failure path of the AI feature needs a test, because those are the bugs customers notice first.

One detail matters early: Supabase pauses free projects after a week of inactivity, so move to a paid plan before real customers depend on the app.

Freelancer, agency or no-code: which fits your budget?

Each option buys you something different. Here is how I would choose.

OptionBest forWatch out for
No-code or AI app builderTesting demand with a clickable product before paying for codeLimits on data, permissions and integrations; you may rebuild later
FreelancerOne clear workflow, when you can make product decisions quicklyOne person is a single point of failure, so insist on code and account ownership
AgencyLarger scope that needs design, QA and project managementPart of the price pays for process; ask who actually writes the code

My honest opinion: if you are not sure anyone will pay, start with no-code or a tiny freelance build. If you already have paying customers waiting, spend the money on solid roles, billing and data design, because those are the hardest parts to change later.

How to cut SaaS MVP cost without cutting quality

The cheapest MVP is the one with fewer features, not the one with cheaper code. These are the cuts I recommend most often:

  • Launch with one user type. Add teams and roles once customers ask for them.
  • Use Stripe Checkout and the Customer Portal. Do not build your own billing screens in version one.
  • Use managed auth. Clerk or Supabase Auth gives you login, password reset and sessions in days instead of weeks.
  • Ship one AI feature, done well. One feature with checks beats three that fail randomly.
  • Skip the admin panel. The database and Stripe dashboards are enough for your first customers.
  • Start with a responsive web app. On Apex Logistics, an installable, offline-ready web app gave drivers an app on their phones without separate iOS and Android builds. If offline matters, my notes on Supabase vs Firebase for offline apps will help you pick a backend.

Fixed price or hourly: how to compare quotes

A fixed price works best when the feature list is clear and written down. Hourly or milestone billing suits exploratory work where requirements will change. I often mix the two: a fixed price for the known core and hourly billing for the parts still being discovered.

Timelines vary in the same way. Purrweb puts a narrow SaaS MVP at 2 to 4 months. A small build with one developer can be faster, and every extra role or integration adds time.

Before you compare two quotes, check that each one answers these questions:

  1. Is there a written feature list, with what is excluded?
  2. Who writes the code, and will you own the repository?
  3. Are hosting, Stripe and domain accounts in your name?
  4. How are change requests priced?
  5. How long are bugs fixed for free after launch?
  6. What will the app cost to run every month?

Two quotes with different prices often describe two different products. These questions make the difference visible.

Practical takeaway

Write down the one workflow your first customers will pay for, then list only the features that workflow needs. Price that list, add the monthly running costs, and keep everything else for version two. That is how you keep SaaS MVP cost under control without shipping something fragile.
